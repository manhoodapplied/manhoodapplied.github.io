A bad social media feed rarely announces itself as a problem. It simply leaves you more agitated, distracted, envious, cynical, or numb than you were ten minutes earlier. You open an app for one useful reason and emerge carrying six opinions you never asked for.

Deleting every app can help, but it is not the only option. A feed is partly the result of repeated signals: what you follow, watch, replay, search, like, share, and linger over. Platforms also provide controls such as **Not interested**, **Don’t recommend**, keyword filters, history management, and feed refreshes. A useful reset combines those controls with clearer rules for your own attention.

## Decide what each app is for

Write one sentence for every platform you keep. “Instagram is for friends, local events, and design references” is a purpose. “I check it when I am bored” is not.

An app without a job will invent one. It will fill empty moments, supply arguments, and turn curiosity into a forty-minute detour. Give it a narrow role so you can judge whether the feed supports that role.

If you cannot name a useful purpose, remove the app from your phone for two weeks. You do not need to delete the account. The distance will tell you whether the platform was serving you or merely occupying you.

## Unfollow by effect, not by identity

Do not ask whether an account is popular, impressive, or occasionally informative. Ask what repeatedly happens to you after you consume it.

Unfollow or mute accounts that reliably create pointless comparison, manufactured outrage, sexual distraction, status anxiety, or advice you never apply. This is not a moral judgment about the creator. It is an editorial decision about your inputs.

Be equally strict with accounts you follow out of obligation. You can care about a person without receiving every post they make. Muting is useful when unfollowing would create needless social friction.

## Train recommendations deliberately

Scrolling past unwanted content is a weak signal because you still gave it time. Use the explicit controls. Mark posts **Not interested**. Choose **Don’t recommend this channel** when the source itself is the problem. Filter recurring keywords. Remove searches or watched videos that were one-time research rather than lasting interests.

YouTube documents that watch history, search history, likes, dislikes, and explicit feedback influence recommendations. TikTok similarly explains that interactions help shape the For You feed and offers tools including **Not interested**, topic management, keyword filters, and a feed refresh. The exact menus change, but the principle is stable: use the controls the platform gives you instead of hoping the system reads your intentions.

Do this aggressively for seven days. Every unwanted recommendation gets feedback. Every useful account gets intentional engagement. You are creating a cleaner pattern, not performing a one-time purge.

## Stop rewarding the hook

Some posts win your attention precisely because they make you angry. That does not make them useful. If you replay the clip, open the comments, send it to a friend, and return later to argue, your behavior says, “More of this.”

Before engaging, ask: **Do I want this subject to occupy more of my feed tomorrow?** If the answer is no, do not investigate the comments for entertainment. Remove the recommendation and leave.

Treat outrage, thirst traps, humiliation videos, and endless “gender war” arguments as junk food for attention. The problem is not that one piece destroys you. The problem is how easily one becomes the default diet.

## Replace before you restrict

An empty feed will quickly refill. Add better inputs on purpose: people who teach a skill, local groups that lead to real activity, friends you genuinely care about, long-form thinkers who show their work, and creators whose advice produces action.

Save fewer posts and apply more of them. If you collect twenty training tips but follow none, the feed is giving you the feeling of improvement without the cost of practice.

## Add friction at the point of entry

Move social apps off the home screen. Disable nonessential notifications. Log out of the platform you open most automatically. Keep the phone outside the bedroom. Choose one or two viewing windows instead of checking whenever discomfort appears.

These small obstacles create a moment in which you can remember your purpose. The goal is not heroic self-control. It is making the unwanted behavior slightly less automatic.

## Review the result after one week

At the end of seven days, ask three questions: Is the feed more useful? Do I leave it in a better state? Am I spending less time on subjects I claim not to care about?

If the answer is still no, take the stronger step: remove the app, use the web version only, or pause the account. You do not owe an algorithm permanent access to your attention. Keep the tools that support your life. Edit or remove the ones that keep editing you.
